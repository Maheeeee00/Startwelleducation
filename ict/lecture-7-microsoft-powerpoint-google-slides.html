1. Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

  1. Create slides using layouts, placeholders and themes.
  2. Apply design principles: contrast, alignment, repetition, proximity and white space.
  3. Insert images, icons, charts and SmartArt appropriately.
  4. Use transitions and animations with restraint.
  5. Prepare speaker notes and deliver using Presenter View within a time limit.

Relevant CLOs: CLO 3 – Apply ICT tools in everyday, academic and professional activities.

2. Introduction

Presentations are part of almost every university course, job interview and business meeting. Software makes it easy to fill slides with text, colours and spinning animations, but that is exactly what makes many presentations hard to follow. A slide is a visual aid for a speaker, not a document to be read. In this lecture we learn the tools of PowerPoint and Google Slides, and the design and delivery principles that make an audience listen.

Roman Urdu: Presentation ka maqsad audience tak baat pohanchana hai, slide par sab kuch likhna nahi. Kam text, bari font, saaf tasveer aur ek slide par ek idea. Speaker notes mein apni tafseel likhein aur presenter view se present karein. Transitions kam se kam rakhein.

3. Detailed explanation

3.1 Presentation software basics

Microsoft PowerPoint (desktop, web and mobile) and Google Slides (browser, free) both organise a presentation as a sequence of slides. Each slide is built from a layout, such as Title Slide, Title and Content, Two Content or Section Header, which contains placeholders for text, images and charts. A theme applies consistent colours, fonts and backgrounds to all slides. The Slide Master (View → Slide Master) controls the design of every layout at once, for example adding a university logo to all slides. Views include Normal for editing, Slide Sorter for reordering and Slide Show for presenting.

Key points
  • Layouts and placeholders
  • Themes and Slide Master
  • Normal, Slide Sorter, Slide Show views
  • Files: .pptx; Slides saves to Drive

3.2 Design principles

Good slide design follows a few principles. Contrast: dark text on a light background or the reverse, so it can be read from the back of the room on an old projector. Alignment: line up text and images along invisible edges. Repetition: keep fonts, colours and title positions consistent. Proximity: place related items together. White space: leave empty space so the slide breathes. Use sans-serif fonts such as Calibri, Arial or Segoe UI, at least 28 point for body text and 36–44 for titles. Limit each slide to one main idea and about six lines of text; the ‘6×6 guideline’ suggests no more than six bullets of six words each.

Key points
  • Contrast, Alignment, Repetition, Proximity
  • White space
  • Body text ≥ 28 pt; titles 36–44 pt
  • One idea per slide; about 6×6 text

3.3 Visuals: images, charts and diagrams

People remember pictures better than text. Use high-quality images that support the message, not decoration, and only images you are allowed to use (PowerPoint’s stock images or free licence sources). Charts should be simple: one message per chart, a clear title stating the takeaway such as ‘Grocery sales doubled in Ramadan’, and labels large enough to read. Charts can be inserted from Excel or created in Slides from Sheets. SmartArt (PowerPoint) or diagrams (Slides) show processes, cycles and hierarchies, for example the information processing cycle. Icons can replace words for simple ideas.

Key points
  • Relevant, high-quality, licensed images
  • Simple charts with a takeaway title
  • SmartArt / diagrams for processes
  • Icons for simple concepts

3.4 Transitions and animations

A transition is the effect between slides; an animation moves an object on a slide. Used sparingly, animation can reveal bullets one at a time so the audience listens instead of reading ahead, or show the steps of a process. Overused, it distracts and wastes time. Choose one subtle transition such as Fade for the whole presentation and use animations only when they explain something, for example ‘Appear’ for each step of a flowchart. Avoid sound effects and spinning or bouncing effects in academic presentations.

Key points
  • Transition: between slides (Fade, Push)
  • Animation: objects on a slide (Appear, Fly in)
  • Use to reveal steps, not to decorate
  • Avoid sounds and flashy effects

3.5 Speaker notes and delivery

Speaker notes are written below each slide in the Notes pane and are visible only to the presenter in Presenter View, which also shows the next slide and a timer. Write the points you will say, examples and transitions, so the slide itself can stay short. Rehearse aloud with a timer; a common rule is about one to two minutes per slide. During delivery, face the audience rather than the screen, speak slowly and clearly, point to what matters, and finish with a summary slide and invite questions. Before presenting on another computer, export a PDF backup and embed fonts or use standard ones.

Key points
  • Notes pane and Presenter View
  • Rehearse with a timer
  • Face the audience; do not read slides
  • End with summary and questions
  • Keep a PDF backup

4. Technical terminology

Layout
An arrangement of placeholders on a slide.
Placeholder
A box on a layout that holds text, images or charts.
Theme
A set of colours, fonts and effects applied to all slides.
Slide Master
The top-level slide that controls the design of all layouts.
Transition
A visual effect when moving from one slide to the next.
Animation
A visual effect applied to an object on a slide.
Speaker notes
Notes for the presenter that the audience does not see.
Presenter View
A view showing notes, timer and next slide to the presenter.

Important definitions

Presentation software: Application software used to create a sequence of slides for displaying information to an audience.
Visual hierarchy: Arranging elements so the most important information is noticed first.
Rehearsal: Practising a presentation aloud with timing before delivering it.

5. Step-by-step example

Worked example: turning a text-heavy slide into a good one

  1. Original slide: 12 lines of a paragraph about Bhimber’s history in 18-point font.
  2. Identify the single main idea: ‘Bhimber is a gateway to Kashmir with a long Mughal history’.
  3. Make that idea the slide title in a short phrase.
  4. Replace the paragraph with a photo of Baghsar Fort and three short bullets.
  5. Move all the detailed history into the speaker notes.
  6. Increase the bullet font to 28 point and align image and text.
  7. Add an Appear animation so each bullet shows when you talk about it.

6. Pakistan-based examples

Final-year project defence

BS students in Pakistan defend their final-year projects with a presentation before a panel. Panels expect clear problem statements, diagrams of the system, demo screenshots and strict timing, often 10–15 minutes.

Job interviews and business pitches

Startup competitions and incubators at Pakistani universities require short pitch decks: problem, solution, market, team and ask.

7. Bhimber case study

Case study: ‘Mera Shehar Bhimber’ presentation

A student must present ‘Mera Shehar Bhimber’ in two minutes to the class. Her first draft has 12 slides, long paragraphs, five different fonts and sound effects. Using the design principles, she reduces it to five slides: a title slide with a photo, location and access, history (Baghsar Fort), education and markets, and a closing slide with three facts and a question for the audience. Details move to speaker notes. She rehearses three times with a timer and presents in 1 minute 55 seconds.

Questions for analysis
  1. Which design principles did she apply?
  2. Why is five slides better than twelve for two minutes?
  3. What should her closing slide contain?

8. Real-life applications

  • Class presentations and project defences
  • Teaching and training sessions
  • Business pitches and reports
  • Seminars and conferences
  • Awareness campaigns in schools

9. Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages

  • Visual support helps audience understanding and memory
  • Consistent themes make work look professional
  • Speaker notes support confident delivery
  • Slides can be shared and reused

Disadvantages

  • Too much text turns slides into reading material
  • Animations can distract
  • Dependence on projector and electricity
  • Design takes time to learn

13. Lecture summary

  • Slides support the speaker; they are not documents.
  • Use layouts, themes and the Slide Master for consistency.
  • Follow contrast, alignment, repetition, proximity and white space.
  • Use visuals and animations only when they explain something.
  • Write speaker notes, rehearse with a timer and keep a PDF backup.

16. Assignment

Prepare a 6–8 slide presentation on ‘ICT in my field of study’ using a consistent theme, one chart, one diagram, at least two relevant images with sources, speaker notes on every slide and no more than 30 words per slide. Present it in five minutes and submit the PPTX and a PDF.

Homework

Find a poorly designed slide online or from an old presentation and redesign it. Submit before and after screenshots with three lines explaining the changes.
